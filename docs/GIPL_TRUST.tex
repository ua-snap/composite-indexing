{\small\color{ink2}\MakeUppercase{Data quality summary}\hfill October 2026\par}
\vspace{3pt}
{\fontsize{17}{20}\selectfont\bfseries Can we trust the GIPL 2.0 permafrost projections?\par}
\vspace{2pt}
{\color{ink2}GIPL 2.0 ground temperature projections for Alaska (SNAP, University of Alaska Fairbanks): 1\,km,
2021--2120, three climate inputs (5-model average, GFDL-CM3, NCAR-CCSM4), RCP 4.5 and 8.5.\par}
\vspace{5pt}

\noindent\colorbox{boxbg}{\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep}{\raggedright\vspace{2pt}%
{\bfseries Short answer: not as delivered.}
We compared GIPL 2.0 with a satellite-based product (ESA CCI Permafrost), 90 boreholes and about 3,000 CUSP field
sites, and checked how the model behaves over time. We found four problems:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\textcolor{accent}{\arabic*}}, leftmargin=1.2em, topsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Stuck cells.} Across 46,000\,km², mostly in the Interior, the ground from 1\,m down never warms in
    100 years while the surface does.
  \item \textbf{Too cold.} About 1--2\,°C colder than every independent reference, by different amounts in different
    places.
  \item \textbf{Start-up and duplicated runs.} The first years jump about 2\,°C, and through 2035, runs that should
    differ do not.
  \item \textbf{Out of step with the air.} GIPL's warming does not follow the air temperature projections for the
    same scenarios.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{1pt}
These problems are in every model and scenario. The run is undocumented, so they can be neither explained nor
corrected.\vspace{2pt}}}

\problem{1}{Stuck cells: ground that never warms}
In 1,172 patches covering \textbf{46,000\,km² (4.6\,\% of Alaska's permafrost area)}, GIPL's temperature at 1\,m
changes by less than 0.1\,°C from 2021 to 2120, under every climate model and scenario. \textbf{88\,\% of this area is
in the Interior, where it covers 10\,\% of the permafrost area, or 19\,\% with the 2\,km border.}
The ground temperature is also stuck at every deeper level we checked (2, 3 and 5\,m), and 0.5\,m warms only 0.4\,°C over the
century (about 5\,°C elsewhere). Each cell holds its own fixed value (−5 to −1\,°C at 1\,m). Yet their surface warms \emph{faster} than nearby cells' (1.5--3.5\,°C
warmer by 2081--2100), and cells within 2\,km are partly affected. Satellite data show nothing unusual
there, and soil and land-cover maps do not explain the pattern.
\fig{\linewidth}{figures/gipl_trust_1_stuck.pdf}
\figcap{\textbf{Figure 1.} (a) Cells where 1\,m temperature changes by < 0.1\,°C, 2021--2120. (b, c) A stuck and a
normal cell 4\,km apart (5-model average, RCP 8.5).}

\problem{2}{Too cold, by different amounts in different places}
Three independent checks agree:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Satellite-based product (ESA CCI):} allowing for about 1\,°C of warming between the two products'
    decades, GIPL is 1.2\,°C colder at the surface and 1.9\,°C colder at 1 and 2\,m (statewide median), and colder in
    82--91\,\% of permafrost cells (Fig.~2a).
  \item \textbf{90 monitoring boreholes} (mostly 2015--2024): GIPL is 2.2, 2.1 and 1.6\,°C too cold at the surface,
    1\,m and 2\,m; ESA CCI is within 0.5\,°C (Fig.~2b). Even GIPL's 2026--2035 values, a decade later, are colder than
    what the boreholes measured.
  \item \textbf{About 3,000 field sites} from the CUSP database, where crews recorded whether permafrost was
    present: at 1\,m, GIPL shows frozen ground at 71\,\% of the 1,532 sites where none was found (ESA CCI: 19\,\%;
    Fig.~2c).
\end{itemize}
The error varies by about 3\,°C from cell to cell and is largest (2--3\,°C) near 0\,°C (the frozen/thawed
threshold). No single correction can remove it.
\fig{\linewidth}{figures/gipl_trust_2_cold.pdf}
\figcap{\textbf{Figure 2.} (a) GIPL 2026--2035 minus ESA CCI 2014--2023 at 1\,m, after removing the projected warming
in between (RCP 8.5). (b) Measured vs.\ dataset temperature, same years. (c) CUSP field sites where crews found no permafrost.}

\problem{3}{An unexplained start-up, and runs that should differ but do not}
GIPL starts in 2021 far from where it settles. At 1\,m the statewide median climbs 1.8\,°C in two years (Fig.~3a);
real ground does not warm that fast. The starting points are mixed up: the 5-model average's two
scenarios start from different ground temperatures although their climates are still nearly the same in 2021
(Fig.~3a), yet four of the six runs (5-model average and GFDL-CM3 under RCP 4.5, NCAR-CCSM4 under both) start from
identical values in every cell (Fig.~3b). In 2023 the NCAR-CCSM4 run's surface spikes to +3.6\,°C (RCP 4.5) and
+5.1\,°C (RCP 8.5), then drops back (Fig.~3b). Its air temperature shows no such jump: under RCP 8.5, 2023 is the
coldest year of 2021--2035. Then, \textbf{through 2035, runs that should differ share one sequence of warm and cold
years:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item GFDL-CM3 (one climate model) is \textbf{identical} to the 5-model average under RCP 4.5, in every cell and
    year (Fig.~3c).
  \item GFDL-CM3 under RCP 8.5 repeats nearly the same values one year earlier (typically within 0.05\,°C; Fig.~3d).
  \item The 5-model average's two scenarios rise and fall together (correlation 0.97; Fig.~3a), although their air temperatures
    do not (−0.08).
\end{itemize}
The runs separate only from 2036. GIPL's 2021--2035 values are therefore not projections for a particular model or
scenario, and comparing them with observations is unreliable.
\fig{\linewidth}{figures/gipl_trust_3_startup.pdf}
\figcap{\textbf{Figure 3.} Statewide median of permafrost cells (stuck cells excluded). (a) Start-up at 1\,m, then
lockstep. (b) NCAR-CCSM4's surface (solid) spikes in 2023; its air temperature (dashed) does not. (c, d) Until 2035,
GFDL-CM3 matches the 5-model average: exactly under RCP 4.5, one year apart under RCP 8.5.}

\problem{4}{Out of step with the air}
\noindent\begin{minipage}[t]{0.505\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}\raggedright
GIPL converts air temperature and snow into ground temperature, so its warming should broadly follow the air warming
of each scenario, damped or amplified by snow. It does not:
\begin{itemize}
  \item From 2036 to 2070, GIPL's surface warms 0.5\,°C per decade under \emph{both} scenarios, while SNAP's air
    projections warm 0.3 (RCP 4.5) and 0.9\,°C (RCP 8.5) per decade (Fig.~4).
  \item By 2081--2100, the extra 2.6\,°C of air warming under RCP 8.5 shows up as only 1.5\,°C of extra warming at
    the surface and 0.9\,°C at 1\,m.
  \item GIPL's year-to-year swings barely follow the air's (median correlation per cell between −0.45 and +0.49).
\end{itemize}
Snow explains part of this: the projected air warming is strongest in winter, under the snowpack. The rest may mean
that GIPL was driven by different climate data than SNAP's published air temperature projections for the same
models. Without documentation this cannot be checked.
\end{minipage}\hfill

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent\colorbox{boxbg}{\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep}{\raggedright\vspace{3pt}%
\textbf{What still holds.} GIPL's spatial pattern is sound: it ranks colder and warmer places as well as ESA CCI does
when checked against the field sites. Its long-term warming is plausible in size. Two workarounds reduce the damage
but do not remove it. Masking stuck cells and their 2\,km border drops about 9\,\% of Alaska's permafrost area and
cannot rule out milder effects elsewhere. Using only GIPL's change over time, added to an observation-based starting level,
removes the cold bias but keeps problems 3 and 4.\par\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Bottom line.} GIPL 2.0 ground temperatures should not be used as absolute values (how cold the ground is, or
whether it is frozen) anywhere in Alaska, and its projected changes only with the caveats above. The problems sit in
the delivered data, and their causes cannot be identified: no documentation exists for this 1\,km run (the only
method reference describes an earlier, coarser run), including its climate inputs, starting conditions, and soil and
snow settings.\vspace{3pt}}}

\textbf{Sources and methods.} GIPL 2.0 (SNAP, University of Alaska Fairbanks): mean annual ground temperature at the
surface, 0.5, 1 and 2\,m, from SNAP's source GeoTIFFs (identical to its Rasdaman service).
ESA CCI Permafrost v5.0, 1\,km, 2003--2023. SNAP 2\,km downscaled air temperature (CMIP5 5-model average and NCAR-CCSM4; CRU TS 4.08
for observed years). Boreholes: Thermal State of Permafrost in North America, UAF Permafrost Laboratory (Arctic Data
Center); 90 Alaska sites with at least one full year of data, outside stuck cells and their border. Field sites: CUSP v1.2
(CommUnity near-Surface Permafrost), 3,012 GIPL cells with records from 2000 on and searches reaching at least 1\,m.
Stuck cell: annual 1\,m temperature range over 2021--2120 below 0.1\,°C in at least one run (97\,\% of the area is
stuck in all six); 2, 3 and 5\,m checked in the 5-model average, RCP 8.5. Regions: USDA general soil map
physiography. Comparisons use permafrost cells (ESA CCI permafrost fraction > 0) outside stuck cells and their
2\,km border (724,326 cells) and report medians. Because GIPL and ESA CCI describe different decades, the projected
air warming between them (about +1\,°C) is removed before comparing; the borehole result holds without this step.
A borehole or field site is a point and a cell is 1\,km², so single sites can differ from their cell by several
degrees; medians over many sites are the meaningful numbers. ESA CCI is itself model-based and not error-free (about
0.5\,°C on average against the boreholes). Ranking skill against field sites (area under the ROC curve): GIPL 0.81, ESA CCI
0.80--0.82. Detailed technical notes are available on request.\par}
